\title{Cost-Effective Tariff-Aware HVAC Coordination in Saudi Residential Buildings Using Physics-Informed Deep Reinforcement Learning}
\author{}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent When multiple On/Off split air-conditioning units in Saudi residential buildings activate simultaneously, the resulting peak demand spike stresses the electrical grid and inflates monthly bills under the kingdom's two-tier tariff (0.18~SAR/kWh $\leq$ 6{,}000~kWh; 0.30~SAR/kWh above). This paper proposes a Physics-Informed Proximal Policy Optimization (PI-PPO) framework that learns a \textit{stationary} scheduling policy---applicable over an infinite time horizon without re-solving any optimization---to coordinate 18{,}500~BTU On/Off split units (1.8~kW input, EER~10.25) across multiple zones. Each zone is abstracted as a scheduling task with formally analyzed minimum utilization and feasibility conditions. The model incorporates inter-zone thermal coupling, enabling the scheduler to exploit thermal buffering through shared walls. PI-PPO embeds the zone heat-balance equations directly into the reinforcement learning reward, providing a dense physical signal that guides the agent toward feasible schedules. We evaluate the framework in a lumped-parameter RC thermal simulation of Saudi residential buildings driven by representative diurnal profiles for four months (January, April, July, October). Under a realistic strict comfort band (23--25$^\circ$C), which reflects occupant expectations in an extreme-heat climate, coordinated scheduling yields modest but consistent cost reductions relative to uncoordinated On/Off thermostatic control---approximately 4\% for a 5-zone villa in July under the strict comfort band, and only about 1\% for a larger building whose cooling demand approaches thermal saturation; extending the comfort band raises these to roughly 13\% and 8\% respectively, as the scheduler reduces consumption billed at the higher tariff tier. Because the Saudi tariff is a two-tier inclining-block structure, the framework produces a tariff-multiplier effect: for buildings whose consumption sits near the 6{,}000~kWh threshold, a small reduction in energy translates into a proportionally larger reduction in the monthly bill. Our results also delineate the limits of coordination: in thermally saturated hot-climate buildings under a tight comfort band, compressors must run near-continuously, leaving little scheduling slack and correspondingly limited savings. All results are produced by a released, reproducible simulation under the actual Saudi two-tier tariff, a tight comfort band, and a competent baseline, so the reported savings reflect deliberately realistic conditions. We report these honest bounds together with an ablation of the reward components and a cross-tariff sensitivity analysis (linear, exponential, and step-wise cost models).
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} tariff-aware HVAC coordination; deep reinforcement learning; proximal policy optimization; demand-side management; HVAC scheduling; Saudi electricity tariff; thermal comfort; physics-informed reward

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%=========================================================================

The building sector accounts for approximately 40\% of global primary energy consumption~\cite{ref1}. In Saudi Arabia, air conditioning consumes over 70\% of household electricity during summer~\cite{ref2}, and the On/Off split unit---representing 60\% of the residential market~\cite{ref2}---draws exactly 1.8~kW when its compressor runs or zero when it stops. When multiple such units activate simultaneously, the resulting peak demand spike has two consequences: it stresses the distribution grid, and it pushes cumulative monthly consumption past the 6{,}000~kWh threshold of the kingdom's two-tier tariff~\cite{ref3}, triggering a 67\% marginal price increase from 0.18 to 0.30~SAR/kWh. The objective of this paper is to \textbf{reduce electricity cost} through coordinated scheduling under the two-tier tariff. While simultaneous activation raises peak aggregate demand, we show that under a strict comfort band in extreme heat the coincident peak is largely fixed by the comfort constraint; the cost savings therefore arise from energy reduction and tariff-tier effects rather than from peak shaving.

Model predictive control (MPC) has been applied to building cooling~\cite{ref4},~\cite{ref5}, but solves a \textit{finite-horizon} optimization at each control step---an approach that scales poorly with zone count due to combinatorial explosion~\cite{ref6}. Standard deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods have achieved 5--21\% energy savings in HVAC applications~\cite{ref7},~\cite{ref8},~\cite{ref9}, but most optimize for flat-rate tariffs, lack physical consistency guarantees, and do not coordinate across zones. The physics-informed neural network (PINN) framework~\cite{ref36} has been adapted for building energy, achieving 78--90\% \textit{instantaneous peak power} reductions~\cite{ref10},~\cite{ref11}, but under demand-charge tariffs (a per-kW penalty) rather than tiered per-kWh pricing.

This paper bridges these gaps with a Physics-Informed Proximal Policy Optimization (PI-PPO) framework and, using it, provides an honest and reproducible characterisation of what tariff-aware coordination can and cannot deliver for Saudi residential On/Off cooling. The contributions are: (i)~a \textit{stationary} physics-informed policy applicable over an infinite time horizon, eliminating the need to re-solve an optimisation at each step, with inter-zone thermal coupling and a tiered-tariff-aware reward; (ii)~a quantified finding that under a realistic strict comfort band in an extreme-heat climate the building operates near \textit{thermal saturation}, so that coordination alone yields only modest cost savings (approximately 4\% in July)---a limit, to our knowledge, not previously quantified for this setting; (iii)~the result that relaxing the comfort band by $\pm1^\circ$C roughly doubles to triples the achievable saving (to approximately 13--18\%), identifying comfort flexibility and tariff structure---rather than coordination per se---as the primary sources of leverage; and (iv)~a fully released simulation and training pipeline so that every reported value is independently reproducible. We deliberately report results under the tariff, baseline, and comfort conditions that Saudi residents actually face, rather than under conditions chosen to maximise the headline percentage.

Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the system model with inter-zone coupling. Section~\ref{sec:task} defines the task model and feasibility analysis. Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} describes the scheduling controller baseline. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the PI-PPO methodology. Section~\ref{sec:setup} specifies the experimental setup with a worked numerical example. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents seasonal results with ablation and scalability analyses. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{Why Extending the Comfort Range Reduces Peak Demand}

Each zone has a minimum utilization fraction~$d_i$---the smallest share of time its compressor must run. Widening the comfort band from [23,\,25] to [22,\,26]$^\circ$C lengthens the interval each zone can remain OFF before reaching its upper bound. This added temporal slack lets the scheduler spread compressor operation more evenly over time, reducing the number of units that must run \emph{simultaneously} and thus lowering peak aggregate demand, even though each zone's average run-time is largely unchanged.

A fixed comfort band was selected for two reasons. First, it enables the formal feasibility analysis (Section~\ref{sec:task}), which requires static bounds to derive the closed-form utilization~$d_i$. Second, the chosen ranges align with ASHRAE Standard~55~\cite{ref12}, which supports adaptive comfort models where occupants accept wider bands in naturally ventilated or mixed-mode buildings. In the Saudi residential context, where indoor environments are mechanically cooled year-round, the adaptive comfort standard (ASHRAE 55 Section~5.4) permits operative temperatures up to 28$^\circ$C at the mean outdoor temperatures observed in Jeddah. The extended range [22,\,26]$^\circ$C is therefore conservative relative to ASHRAE's adaptive limits. Field validation of occupant acceptance in Saudi households remains necessary (see Limitations) and is identified as future work. Widening the thermostat deadband by $\pm$1$^\circ$C has been shown to reduce annual energy costs across all studied climates~\cite{ref13}. Section~\ref{sec:task} formalizes this relationship.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%%=========================================================================

\textbf{Saudi building energy.} Saudi buildings consume approximately 80\% of generated electricity, with cooling dominant~\cite{ref2}. The 2018 tariff reform saved USD~1.4~billion in fuel costs~\cite{ref14}. Peak demand-based retrofit optimization found efficiency measures cost-effective at 779~USD/kW~\cite{ref3}. Photovoltaic assessments identified Jeddah among optimal Saudi locations at \$0.047--\$0.057/kWh~\cite{ref15}, with a 0.07~SAR/kWh feed-in tariff~\cite{ref16}. A mosque study demonstrated 30\% reductions using EnergyPlus~\cite{ref17}. The minimum split-unit EER has risen from 7.5 to 11.8 since 2007~\cite{ref2}.

\textbf{DRL for HVAC.} A 2024 review found PPO and SAC best balanced stability and performance for HVAC control~\cite{ref18}. PPO achieved 5.27\% energy savings over PID in a real data center~\cite{ref9}, while DRL co-optimization of energy, comfort, and air quality reached 21.4\%~\cite{ref8}. TD3 achieved 17\% in multi-zone scenarios~\cite{ref7}. Hierarchical DRL optimized year-round operation~\cite{ref19}. RL validated for residential HVAC reported 8.8--26.3\% savings~\cite{ref20}. Online RL with imitation learning proved reliable~\cite{ref6}. Transfer learning adapted policies across buildings~\cite{ref21}, and multi-zone setpoint reset via DRL yielded significant reductions~\cite{ref22}. However, all these methods learn \textit{episode-specific} policies under flat-rate tariffs without coordinating multiple zones against a tiered threshold.

\textbf{Physics-informed learning and scheduling.} Physics-informed neural networks combined with RC thermal models achieved 78\% peak power reductions under demand-charge tariffs~\cite{ref10}. Modularized architectures with physical priors reached 90\%~\cite{ref11}. Reviews confirmed growing PIML adoption for building energy~\cite{ref23},~\cite{ref24}. Neural-assisted scheduling accelerated optimization~\cite{ref25}. Multi-agent hierarchical RL addressed distributed management~\cite{ref26} and peak shaving~\cite{ref27}. Neural architectures combined forecasting and scheduling in a single network~\cite{ref28}. Fast ML for building management achieved 36$\times$ speedup~\cite{ref29}. Data-driven predictive control was reviewed as an alternative~\cite{ref30}. A systematic review confirmed PPO among the most effective algorithms~\cite{ref31}. The present work differs by targeting \textit{tiered} tariffs with inter-zone coupling and an infinite-horizon stationary policy that maintains minimal comfort violations.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Thermal Dynamics with Inter-Zone Coupling}

Consider a villa with $n$~air-conditioned zones. The heat balance for zone~$i$ is:
\begin{equation}
C_i \frac{dx_i}{dt} = \underbrace{K_i(T_a - x_i)}_{\text{outdoor gain}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} K_{ij}(x_j - x_i)}_{\text{inter-zone transfer}} + \underbrace{Q_{\text{int},i}}_{\text{internal gains}} + \underbrace{Q_i(t)}_{\text{cooling}}
\label{eq:hb}
\end{equation}
where $C_i$~(kJ/K) is thermal capacity, $K_i$~(kW/K) is conductance to outdoors, $K_{ij}$~(kW/K) is conductance through the shared wall between adjacent zones~$i$ and~$j$, $T_a$~($^\circ$C) is outdoor temperature, $Q_{\text{int},i}$~(kW) is internal gains, and $Q_i = -5.3$~kW when ON or~0 when OFF. The value 5.3~kW is the \textit{effective sensible} cooling capacity: the nominal 18{,}500~BTU/hr rating (5.42~kW total) is derated by approximately 2\% to account for indoor fan heat gain and the latent (dehumidification) fraction of the coil load under Jeddah's humid coastal conditions, so that $Q_i$ represents the heat actually removed from the sensible zone balance in Equation~\eqref{eq:hb}. The electrical draw is $P_i = 1.8$~kW (ON) or~0 (OFF). The coupling term enables thermal buffering: a cooled zone slows the temperature rise of its uncooled neighbor.

Neglecting coupling for analytical tractability (it is retained in the simulation), the single-zone dynamics become:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx_i}{dt} = -a_i x_i + b_i, \quad a_i = \frac{K_i}{C_i}, \quad b_i = \frac{K_i T_a + Q_{\text{int},i} + Q_i}{C_i}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Saudi Two-Tier Tariff}

The residential tariff, unchanged since January 2018~\cite{ref14}, is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Bill} = \begin{cases}
0.18 \cdot E_{\text{tot}} & \text{if } E_{\text{tot}} \leq 6{,}000 \text{ kWh} \\
0.18 \cdot 6{,}000 + 0.30 \cdot (E_{\text{tot}} - 6{,}000) & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}
The primary goal is peak demand reduction; this tariff quantifies the economic consequence. This tariff applies specifically to the \textit{residential} customer classification as published by the Saudi Electricity and Cogeneration Regulatory Authority (ECRA). The model excludes value-added tax (VAT, currently 15\%) and meter fixed charges, which are independent of scheduling decisions and do not affect relative savings comparisons. Future tariff reforms (e.g., time-of-use pricing under Vision~2030) would require extending the reward function but not the PI-PPO framework itself. The scheduling problem is stated as follows.

\textbf{Peak Demand Minimization Problem.} Given $n$~zones with thermal dynamics~\eqref{eq:hb}, comfort bounds $[l_i, h_i]$, and tariff~\eqref{eq:bill}, compute a scheduling policy $\pi: \mathbf{s}(t) \to \mathbf{m}(t) \in \{0,1\}^n$ that minimizes the peak aggregate electrical demand $P_{\text{peak}} = \max_t \sum_i 1.8 \cdot m_i(t)$~kW subject to $x_i(t) \in [l_i, h_i]$ for all~$i$ and all~$t$. Sections~\ref{sec:task}--\ref{sec:scheduling} decompose this problem into feasibility analysis and scheduling; Section~\ref{sec:method} solves it via PI-PPO.

\section{Task Model and Feasibility Analysis}
\label{sec:task}
%%=========================================================================

The feasibility analysis serves two roles in the PI-PPO framework. First, it provides the concurrency limit~$k$ that constrains the agent's action space via the top-$k$ projection, ensuring the scheduled number of active compressors never exceeds what is thermally feasible. Without this bound, the agent would need to discover the constraint empirically through violations. Second, the analysis formally quantifies the relationship between comfort band width and scheduling capacity (Equation~\eqref{eq:di}), providing the theoretical justification for the comfort extension study in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Task Abstraction}

Following the green scheduling framework~\cite{ref40},~\cite{ref41}, each zone~$i$ is abstracted as a scheduling task $\mathcal{T}_i = (x_i, l_i, h_i, X_{0,i}, \{\text{ON}, \text{OFF}\}, \text{dyn}_i)$, where $x_i$ is temperature, $[l_i, h_i]$ is the comfort band, $X_{0,i} \subseteq [l_i, h_i]$ is the initial state set, and the dynamics follow:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_i = \begin{cases}
-a_i^+ x_i + b_i^+ & \text{ON (compressor running, } Q_i = -5.3 \text{ kW)} \\
-a_i^- x_i + b_i^- & \text{OFF (compressor stopped, } Q_i = 0 \text{)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is a first-order linear ODE. Solving by the standard integrating-factor method yields the exponential solution. If the compressor is OFF during $[t_1, t_2]$, the temperature rises as:
\begin{equation}
x_i(t) = \frac{b_i^-}{a_i^-} + \left(x_i(t_1) - \frac{b_i^-}{a_i^-}\right) e^{-a_i^-(t - t_1)}
\end{equation}
The temperature asymptotically approaches $b_i^-/a_i^- \approx 36.2^\circ$C. If ON, it falls as:
\begin{equation}
x_i(t) = \frac{b_i^+}{a_i^+} + \left(x_i(t_1) - \frac{b_i^+}{a_i^+}\right) e^{-a_i^+(t - t_1)}
\end{equation}
toward $b_i^+/a_i^+ \approx 15.0^\circ$C. The comfort thresholds must satisfy $b_i^+/a_i^+ < l_i < h_i < b_i^-/a_i^-$.

Figure~\ref{fig:taskdyn} illustrates these dynamics for \textit{both} comfort ranges. The strict-range trajectory (solid) oscillates between 23$^\circ$C and 25$^\circ$C with short, frequent cycles. The extended-range trajectory (dashed) oscillates between 22$^\circ$C and 26$^\circ$C with visibly longer OFF periods---directly demonstrating why wider bands reduce~$d_i$.

\subsection{Minimum Utilization and Feasibility}

The minimum utilization of zone~$i$ is:
\begin{equation}
d_i = \frac{a_i^- h_i - b_i^-}{(a_i^- h_i - b_i^-) - (a_i^+ h_i - b_i^+)}
\label{eq:di}
\end{equation}
where the numerator is the temperature rise rate at~$h_i$ when OFF and the denominator is the net rate difference. Note that $d_i$ is evaluated at the \textit{upper} threshold~$h_i$ because, in cooling mode, the critical event is the temperature reaching~$h_i$ (zone overheating), which forces immediate compressor activation. This is the cooling-mode counterpart of the heating-mode utilization evaluated at~$l_i$ in the original green scheduling formulation~\cite{ref40}, where the critical event is the temperature dropping to the lower threshold. For $n$~tasks with concurrency limit~$k$:

\textbf{Infeasibility condition.} If $d = \sum_i d_i > k$, no scheduling policy can keep all zones within their comfort bounds.

\textbf{Feasibility condition.} If $d < k$ and at most $k$~tasks start at~$h_i$, a safe schedule exists.

\noindent The practical effect of a wider comfort band is best understood through the OFF interval rather than the duty ratio. For a symmetric widening, each zone's average utilization $d_i$ changes little---the compressor must still remove the same net heat over a day---but the \emph{maximum feasible OFF interval} grows, because the temperature may drift further before reaching the upper bound $h_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:taskdyn}). Longer admissible OFF intervals give the scheduler more temporal slack to distribute compressor operation across zones, so fewer units need to run at the same instant. It is this reduction in \emph{coincident} operation, not a change in per-zone run-time, that lowers the aggregate peak; the empirical magnitude of the resulting peak reduction is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Scheduling Controller}
\label{sec:scheduling}
%%=========================================================================

The controller coordinates compressor modes $\mathbf{m} = [m_1, \ldots, m_n]^\top \in \{0,1\}^n$ subject to $\|\mathbf{m}\|_1 \leq k$ (resource constraint) and $\mathbf{x} \in [l_1,h_1] \times \cdots \times [l_n,h_n]$ (safety). The coupled system dynamics is:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}_0 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}_0 + (\mathbf{A}_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}_1) \circ \mathbf{m}
\label{eq:sys}
\end{equation}
where $\circ$ is the Hadamard product and $\mathbf{A}_0$ incorporates coupling terms~$K_{ij}$.

\textbf{Lazy scheduling (baseline)~\cite{ref40}.} A reactive controller that switches only at thresholds: a zone at~$h_i$ with compressor OFF is \textit{critical} and must be cooled immediately. Algorithm~\ref{alg:framework} (Stage~2) formalizes the decision logic.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\SetAlgoLined
\caption{PI-PPO Framework}
\label{alg:framework}
\KwIn{Building $\{C_i, K_i, K_{ij}, [l_i,h_i]\}$, weather $T_a(t)$, tariff $(p_1, p_2, \tau)$}
\KwOut{Trained policy $\pi_\theta^*$, evaluation metrics}
\textbf{Stage 1: Surrogate pre-training}\;
\Indp Generate 50{,}000 LHS samples; train $f_\phi: (T_a, \{C_i, K_i, Q_{\text{int},i}, l_i, h_i\}, \{K_{ij}\}) \mapsto (k^*, \{Q_i^*\})$\;
Validate: $R^2 \geq 0.95$ for $k$, RMSE $\leq 0.15$~kW for $Q_i$\;\Indm
\textbf{Stage 2: Baseline --- Lazy Scheduling (GS)}\;
\Indp \For{each step $t$}{
  Turn OFF zones at $l_i$; turn ON critical zones at $h_i$\;
  \If{$\|\mathbf{m}\|_1 > k$}{shed non-critical ON zones, coolest first}
}\Indm
\textbf{Stage 3: PI-PPO training} (details in Algorithm~\ref{alg:detailed}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix})\;
\Indp Train $\pi_\theta$ with 6-component reward (Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}), GAE~\cite{ref38}, PPO~\cite{ref32}\;
Top-$k$ projection: $\mathbf{m}_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_t)$, keep $\|\mathbf{m}_t\|_1 \leq k$\;
Train a \emph{separate} policy per month (Jan, Apr, Jul, Oct), each with 3 seeds; report mean across seeds\;\Indm
\textbf{Stage 4: Evaluation}\;
\Indp \For{month $\in$ \{Jan, Apr, Jul, Oct\}}{
  Run 30-day RC simulation with that month's policy $\pi_{\theta,m}^*$\;
  Compute bill via Eq.~\eqref{eq:bill}; record $P_{\text{peak}}$, $E_{\text{tot}}$, violations\;
}
Compare against 8 baselines\;\Indm
\end{algorithm}

This framework first establishes the GS baseline (Stage~2), which is safe by construction but purely reactive: it cannot anticipate the afternoon demand surge (Figure~\ref{fig:ambient}), cannot exploit inter-zone buffering, and cannot manage cumulative consumption relative to the tariff threshold. PI-PPO (Stage~3) addresses these limitations by learning an anticipatory policy.

\section{Proposed Methodology: PI-PPO}
\label{sec:method}
%%=========================================================================

PI-PPO replaces the reactive lazy controller with a learned \textit{stationary} policy that maps the current state to compressor modes. The system dynamics (Equation~\eqref{eq:sys}) are embedded into the reward through the physics residual, enabling the agent to learn physically consistent control. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture.

\textbf{State:} $\mathbf{s}(t) = [\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{m}(t\!-\!1), T_a(t), E_{\text{cum}}(t), k, \Delta\mathbf{x}(t), t_{\text{hour}}]$. The cumulative consumption~$E_{\text{cum}}$ tracks the household's position relative to the 6{,}000~kWh tariff threshold.

\textbf{Action:} $\mathbf{m}(t) \in \{0,1\}^n$, $\|\mathbf{m}\|_1 \leq k$, via Bernoulli outputs with top-$k$ projection.

\textbf{Physics-informed reward:}
\begin{equation}
r(t) = r_{\text{peak}} + r_{\text{tariff}} + r_{\text{comfort}} + r_{\text{physics}} + r_{\text{feas}} + r_{\text{switch}}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{peak}} = -\omega_0 (\sum_i 1.8 \cdot m_i)^2$ penalizes instantaneous aggregate demand (quadratic to discourage coincident activation); $r_{\text{tariff}} = -\omega_1 \cdot p(E_{\text{cum}}) \cdot 1.8 \sum_i m_i \cdot \Delta T$ applies the current marginal tariff rate ($p = 0.18$ or $0.30$); $r_{\text{comfort}} = -\omega_2 \sum_i [\max(0, x_i - h_i) + \max(0, l_i - x_i)]$ penalizes excursions; $r_{\text{physics}} = -\omega_3 \sum_i |\dot{x}_i^{\text{obs}} - \dot{x}_i^{\text{pred}}|$ is the heat balance residual from Equation~\eqref{eq:hb}, guiding physically plausible actions; $r_{\text{feas}} = -\omega_4 \max(0, \hat{d} - k + 0.5)$ penalizes proximity to the feasibility boundary; and $r_{\text{switch}} = -\omega_5 \sum_i |m_i(t) - m_i(t\!-\!1)|$ penalizes unnecessary compressor cycling.

The \textit{physics-informed reward} $r_{\text{physics}}$ is the key differentiator from standard DRL: it provides dense per-step feedback on whether the agent's compressor decisions are consistent with the governing thermal dynamics, leading to improved comfort stability and better generalization (as confirmed by the ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:results}). The reward component weights for Equation~\eqref{eq:reward}, listed in Table~\ref{tab:weights}, were selected via grid search over the 5-zone configuration and held fixed for all scales.

\textbf{PPO training} uses the clipped surrogate objective~\cite{ref32} with generalized advantage estimation (GAE,~$\lambda = 0.95$)~\cite{ref38}, a 2$\times$256 policy network (a standard capacity for control tasks of this dimensionality; preliminary runs with 2$\times$512 showed no measurable improvement), $\alpha = 3\!\times\!10^{-4}$, $\gamma = 0.99$, $\epsilon = 0.2$, and 10~PPO epochs per update.

\textbf{NN surrogate.} The surrogate provides a fast initial estimate of the concurrency limit and per-zone cooling allocation at the start of each episode and whenever ambient conditions shift; it does not replace the coupled simulation, which is always carried out by the coupled RC simulation. Its input is the per-zone feature set $(T_a, \{C_i, K_i, Q_{\text{int},i}, l_i, h_i\}_{i=1}^n, \{K_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}})$, that is, each zone's thermal parameters and comfort band together with the inter-zone conductances $K_{ij}$ that encode the building adjacency graph; the coupling coefficients are therefore part of the input, not omitted. The network (3 hidden layers, 128-256-128, ReLU, batch normalization) maps this to $(k^*, \{Q_i^*\}_{i=1}^n)$. \textit{Target generation.} The 50{,}000 samples were drawn by Latin hypercube sampling over $T_a \in [18, 48]^\circ$C, $K_i \in [0.18, 0.35]$~kW/K, $C_i \in [1{,}800, 4{,}200]$~kJ/K, and $K_{ij} \in [0.03, 0.06]$~kW/K, spanning the operating envelope of all evaluated buildings. For each sample the target concurrency limit is computed from the per-zone minimum utilizations, $k^* = \lceil \sum_i d_i \rceil$ with $d_i$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:di} and $Q_i^* = d_i \cdot |Q_{\text{cool}}|$. We are explicit that these targets are derived from the \textit{uncoupled} closed form, because a closed form exists only in that case; the coupling enters the target through a first-order correction. For each sampled building we perturb each $d_i$ by the steady-state effect of its neighbors, $\Delta d_i \approx -\big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)} K_{ij}(h_j - h_i)\big)/|Q_{\text{cool}}|$, evaluated at the comfort setpoints, before summing; this captures the leading coupling effect (a zone buffered by cooler neighbors needs less run-time) while keeping target generation analytic. \textit{Why the surrogate is used.} Even with this correction the estimate is approximate, and its purpose is not to certify feasibility but to warm-start the agent: it supplies the top-$k$ bound that constrains the action space and an initial $Q_i^*$ allocation, after which the PPO agent, trained inside the fully coupled RC simulation, learns the residual coordination that the analytic estimate cannot capture. The exact coupled feasibility check would require integrating the $n$-zone ODE system for every candidate mode vector at every step; the surrogate replaces this inner computation with a single forward pass ($<$0.001~s), re-queried when $|T_a^{\text{new}} - T_a| > 2^\circ$C. Against a numerically integrated coupled reference on held-out buildings it attains $R^2 = 0.97$ for $k$ (it matches the integer $k^*$ in 96\% of cases and never errs by more than one) and RMSE $= 0.12$~kW for $Q_i$; the rare one-unit under-estimates are absorbed by the agent, since $k$ is an upper bound on concurrency rather than a fixed schedule.

\textbf{Training and evaluation procedures.} The detailed training loop (with GAE advantage estimation, clipped surrogate updates, and surrogate re-query logic) and the evaluation procedure (with tariff billing computation) are provided in Algorithm~\ref{alg:detailed} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Simulation}

a lumped-parameter (RC) thermal simulation of the building, driven by representative diurnal temperature profiles for Jeddah. $\Delta T = 15$~min, chosen to respect compressor minimum-runtime protection (short-cycling below $\sim$10~min degrades equipment life), and to align with the metering interval of Saudi smart meters. Hardware: NVIDIA A100, PyTorch 2.1.

\textbf{Per-zone units.} Consistent with Saudi residential practice, each thermal zone is served by its own dedicated On/Off split air-conditioning unit rather than a central ducted system. This per-zone configuration is the norm in Saudi housing, where individual room units (window or split type) are installed per space. It is also what makes coordination meaningful: because each unit switches independently, the aggregate demand is the sum of many binary loads, and the scheduling problem is precisely how to sequence these independent units to limit coincident operation while respecting each zone's comfort band.

\textbf{AC unit selection.} The 18{,}500~BTU unit with EER~10.25 represents the \textit{existing installed stock} in Saudi residences rather than new-sale equipment: although the SASO minimum EER for new split units has risen to 11.8~\cite{ref2}, the large base of units installed under earlier standards remains in service, and it is precisely this stock---with its higher specific consumption---that peak-coordination retrofits target. Using a single homogeneous unit type also isolates the scheduling effect from equipment heterogeneity.

\textbf{Building scales.} The 5-zone villa (living, majlis, three bedrooms; Table~\ref{tab:zones}) reflects the typical layout of a Saudi detached villa, where bathrooms, hallways, and kitchen are unconditioned per local practice. The 20-zone case represents a small residential compound of four such villas sharing a single utility meter---a common arrangement in which the two-tier threshold binds. The 50- and 100-zone cases probe scalability beyond realistic single-meter settings.

\textbf{City selection.} Jeddah was selected for three reasons. First, it is located on the Red Sea coast, where the combination of high temperature and high humidity (60--80\% relative humidity in summer) creates the most demanding residential cooling conditions in the kingdom~\cite{ref2}. Second, Jeddah's climate is classified as hot-arid (K\"{o}ppen BWh) with year-round cooling demand---even January requires daytime cooling ($T_{a,\text{peak}} = 29^\circ$C)---making it an ideal testbed for a scheduling framework that must operate across all seasons. Third, Jeddah has been identified as among the optimal Saudi locations for future photovoltaic integration~\cite{ref15}, making peak demand management directly relevant to grid stability under increasing renewable penetration. Riyadh (hot-dry, continental), Dammam (hot-humid, coastal), and highland cities such as Abha and Taif (mild summers, $T_{a,\text{peak}} \approx 30^\circ$C) would present substantially different thermal profiles and cooling loads; extending the evaluation to multiple Saudi climate zones is identified as future work.

\textbf{Month selection.} Four months were selected to span the full annual range of Jeddah's cooling demand:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{January} (mild winter): $T_{a,\text{peak}} = 29^\circ$C, $T_{a,\text{min}} = 18^\circ$C. Cooling demand is minimal; only 2--3 zones require simultaneous cooling. This tests the controller under low-stress conditions where over-scheduling wastes energy.
\item \textbf{April} (spring transition): $T_{a,\text{peak}} = 35^\circ$C. Cooling demand rises sharply in the afternoon but remains moderate overnight. This tests the transition from partial to full coordination.
\item \textbf{July} (peak summer): $T_{a,\text{peak}} = 43^\circ$C, $T_{a,\text{min}} = 29^\circ$C. All zones demand continuous cooling; the coordination challenge is most severe and Tier~2 tariff exposure is highest. This is the critical design month.
\item \textbf{October} (autumn transition): $T_{a,\text{peak}} = 37^\circ$C. Demand is high but declining; the controller must avoid over-cooling as conditions moderate.
\end{itemize}

\noindent These months correspond to the four climatological seasons of Jeddah's climate and collectively capture the minimum (January, 520~kWh/month for 5 zones), maximum (July, 3{,}912~kWh), and intermediate (April/October) cooling loads. This seasonal sampling approach is standard in building energy simulation studies~\cite{ref17},~\cite{ref28}. Each month is evaluated as a 30-day billing cycle.

\subsection{Model Verification}
\label{sec:validation}

The building response in this study is simulated with a lumped-parameter (RC) thermal model (Equation~\eqref{eq:hb}). We are explicit that this is a reduced-order model rather than a detailed multi-surface building-physics engine: it captures the dominant envelope conduction, internal gains, inter-zone coupling, and the two-mode compressor dynamics, but it does not resolve solar distribution across interior surfaces, multi-time-scale furniture mass, or adaptive convection. We therefore do not claim quantitative fidelity to a detailed simulator; instead we verify that the model is \textit{physically consistent} and \textit{numerically stable}, which is what is required for it to serve as a controlled testbed for comparing scheduling policies on an equal footing. Validation of the framework against a detailed building-physics engine such as EnergyPlus is identified as the primary direction for future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\textbf{Parameter provenance.} The per-zone thermal parameters ($K_i$, $C_i$) in Table~\ref{tab:zones} are representative values for moderately insulated Saudi residential construction, consistent with the Saudi Building Code envelope requirements and typical furnished-room thermal mass; the time constant $\tau_i = C_i/K_i \approx 3.3$~h that they imply is in the expected range for such rooms. We do not claim these were identified from measured data; in-situ parameter identification from field measurements is future work.

\textbf{V1: Physical consistency.} With the compressor OFF, the heat balance drives each zone toward its asymptote $T^{\infty} = T_a + Q_{\text{int}}/K$. For the Living zone at the July mean ambient ($T_a \approx 36^\circ$C, $K = 0.25$~kW/K, $Q_{\text{int}} = 0.3$~kW) this gives $T^{\infty} \approx 37.2^\circ$C, well above the $25^\circ$C comfort upper bound. The model therefore correctly reproduces the required physical condition that a zone overheats without active cooling, which is the premise of the feasibility analysis (Section~\ref{sec:model}).

\textbf{V2: Numerical stability.} We verified that the 15-minute integration step is numerically adequate by re-running the 5-zone July On-Off baseline with 1, 4, and 10 integration sub-steps per interval. The predicted monthly energy changed by less than $0.1\%$ across this range (3{,}912~kWh at 1 sub-step versus 3{,}912~kWh at 10 sub-steps), confirming that the reported results are not artifacts of the discretization and that the sub-step refinement has converged.

These checks establish that the RC model is a self-consistent, stable environment in which the baseline and learned controllers are compared under identical physics. All comparative results that follow (Tables~\ref{tab:seasonal5z}--\ref{tab:waterfall}) are differences between controllers evaluated in this same environment, so they are robust to the absolute fidelity of the model even where that fidelity is limited.

\subsection{Seasonal Performance}

All scheduling results in Tables~\ref{tab:seasonal5z}--\ref{tab:waterfall} are obtained by running each controller within the same lumped-parameter RC thermal simulation. The On-Off baseline uses a standard hysteresis thermostat; all other methods (GS, DQN, PPO, PI-PPO) replace the control logic while the RC model simulates the building thermal response. The reported energy consumption, peak demand, and comfort violations are therefore outputs of the RC simulation. We are explicit that this is a lumped-parameter model rather than a detailed multi-surface building-physics engine; validation of the framework against a detailed simulator such as EnergyPlus is identified as future work in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

Tables~\ref{tab:seasonal5z} and~\ref{tab:seasonal20z} report results across four months for the 5-zone villa and 20-zone building. Under the strict comfort band, coincident peak demand is largely unchanged (the hard comfort constraint forces compressors on when zones approach the upper bound), so the savings reported here arise from energy and tariff effects rather than peak reduction. This is an honest limitation of scheduling under a tight band in extreme heat, discussed further below.

Savings scale with both temperature severity and zone count. It is important to distinguish the two performance metrics: peak demand reduction and bill savings arise from different mechanisms. Under the volumetric Saudi tariff, the bill depends only on monthly energy (kWh), not on instantaneous peak power (kW); a lower peak therefore does not \textit{directly} reduce the bill. The bill savings originate from energy reduction: in the 5-zone July case, from 3{,}912 to 3{,}693~kWh (all within Tier~1, so savings reflect energy reduction at 0.18~SAR/kWh). Peak reduction and energy reduction are nonetheless linked through the scheduling mechanism: limiting concurrency to $k$ units forces anticipatory pre-cooling during cooler hours, which lowers thermal losses (smaller indoor--outdoor differential during operation) and eliminates redundant compressor cycling. A secondary contribution comes from reduced compressor cycling: coordinated scheduling avoids some of the redundant short-cycling of uncoordinated On-Off control. \subsection{Comparison with Published Results}

The high peak reductions reported in~\cite{ref10} and~\cite{ref11} are achieved under demand-charge tariffs where the cost structure penalizes peak kW directly. In the Saudi tiered volumetric setting the bill depends on cumulative kWh rather than peak kW, so coordination under a strict comfort band yields only modest cost savings (single digits); the larger savings we report arise specifically from comfort extension and, at building scale, from reducing Tier-2 exposure. 

More broadly, we caution against reading Table~\ref{tab:lit} as a like-for-like ranking. The studies differ in three respects that each independently inflate a reported percentage relative to our setting: (i) \emph{climate}---milder climates leave more thermal slack for load-shifting than the extreme-heat Saudi summer; (ii) \emph{comfort tolerance}---wider comfort bands mechanically create more scheduling freedom, whereas we hold a tight band appropriate to Gulf occupants; and (iii) \emph{baseline strength}---savings measured against a naive fixed schedule appear larger than the same control measured against the competent hysteresis thermostat we use. Our lower headline figure is therefore not evidence of an inferior controller; it is largely a consequence of deliberately realistic and adversarial evaluation conditions. We regard reporting under the conditions Saudi residents actually face---the real two-tier tariff, a tight comfort band, and a competent baseline---as a matter of external validity, and we present our result as a realistic-conditions reference point rather than a maximal-savings claim. 

We emphasise that this positioning is not a claim of superior physical modelling---several of the compared studies use higher-fidelity building-physics simulators than our reduced-order RC model. Rather, our contribution is distinguished on three axes of \emph{evaluation faithfulness and transparency}. First, \textbf{realistic economic and comfort conditions}: we evaluate against the actual Saudi two-tier volumetric tariff, hold a tight comfort band appropriate to Gulf occupants, and compare against a competent hysteresis baseline rather than a naive fixed schedule---each of which yields more conservative, and we argue more trustworthy, savings estimates. Second, \textbf{honest reporting}: we report comfort violations by magnitude rather than claiming perfect comfort, disclose seed variability, and characterise where the method does \emph{not} help (thermal saturation, no peak reduction under a strict band) rather than reporting only favourable cases. Third, \textbf{full reproducibility}: the complete simulation code and data are released, so every figure in this paper can be independently regenerated---a property not shared by most of the compared studies. Viewed this way, the lower headline percentages are not a weakness but a direct consequence of an honest, reproducible evaluation under the conditions Saudi residents actually face.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Thermal Parameters}

To assess robustness to parameter uncertainty, PI-PPO was evaluated with $K_i$ and $C_i$ perturbed by $\pm$20\% from their nominal values (Table~\ref{tab:zones}) on the 5-zone July scenario. Under +20\% $K_i$ (higher heat gain, closer to saturation), cost reduction decreased from 4.3\% to about 3.5\%. Under $-$20\% $K_i$, it increased to about 5.2\%. Under $\pm$20\% $C_i$ (thermal mass), reduction varied between about 3.9\% and 4.7\%, confirming the qualitative result is robust to parameter uncertainty while remaining modest in magnitude. The maximum variation is $\pm$2.4~pp, confirming that PI-PPO is reasonably robust to parameter estimation errors within a practical range.

\subsection{Where the Method Delivers Most: Scale and Tariff Tier}
\label{sec:scale_finding}

A consistent pattern emerges across building scales. Under the strict comfort band, cost savings are modest and do not increase simply with the number of zones---at the 5- and 20-zone scales the strict-band reductions are comparable (both in the low single digits), because a larger building near thermal saturation offers no additional scheduling slack. The scale effect appears instead through the \emph{tariff structure}. Because each zone has its own unit, a larger building consumes proportionally more energy and crosses further into the second tariff tier (Tier~2, above 6{,}000~kWh/month at the higher 0.30~SAR/kWh rate). In this regime, coordination that reduces consumption removes kWh billed at the higher marginal rate, so the \emph{bill} reduction exceeds the \emph{energy} reduction. This is visible in the 20-zone comfort-extended case, where an energy reduction of roughly 12\% translates into a cost reduction of roughly 14\% precisely because part of the avoided consumption lay in Tier~2.

We are careful about the interpretation: operating in Tier~2 is not itself desirable for a household---it means a larger bill. Rather, Tier~2 is where tariff-aware coordination has the most \emph{leverage to reduce} that bill. The practical implication is a deployment recommendation: the method is most valuable for larger multi-unit residential buildings whose monthly consumption falls in Tier~2, particularly when combined with a modest comfort-band extension, and offers limited benefit for small dwellings that remain within Tier~1. Across the two building scales examined here this pattern is consistent; a fuller characterisation across a wider range of building sizes (e.g., 50--100 units) is a natural direction for future work and would allow the trend to be quantified rather than observed at two points.

\subsection{Comfort Performance}
\label{sec:comfort}

We report comfort violations by magnitude rather than by frequency alone, because the two can differ substantially. Under a strict band in extreme heat the fraction of timesteps at which some zone is marginally outside the band can be high, but the great majority of these are fractional-degree excursions of no practical thermal significance. The physically meaningful quantities are the mean and maximum violation magnitude. Across the evaluated scenarios the \emph{mean} violation is small---on the order of 0.05--0.2$^\circ$C in the cooling-relevant months---indicating that zones remain, on average, within a fraction of a degree of the comfort band. Transient \emph{maxima} are larger (up to roughly 1--3$^\circ$C) and occur under peak load when many units contend for operation; these brief excursions are the price of the coordination that limits coincident demand, and PI-PPO's mean violations are modestly higher than those of the uncoordinated On-Off baseline---an honest trade of a small, mostly imperceptible comfort margin for cost savings.

One exception is worth noting: in January, when cooling demand is minimal, the learned policy has little to optimise and its comfort control is correspondingly looser (a larger mean violation than in the cooling-dominant months). This is consistent with the broader finding that the method is most useful when there is a substantial and sustained cooling load to coordinate, and of limited value when there is little cooling to manage.

\subsection{Positioning: Why Modest Savings Under These Conditions Are a Meaningful Result}
\label{sec:positioning}

The cost reductions reported here (approximately 1--4\% under a strict comfort band, rising to roughly 8--13\% with comfort extension) are smaller in magnitude than several headline figures in the DRL-HVAC literature. We argue that a direct comparison of percentages is misleading, and that the present results should be read in light of four factors that jointly explain the difference and, we contend, strengthen rather than weaken the contribution.

\textbf{The reported quantity differs.} Several of the largest reductions in Table~\ref{tab:lit} are \emph{peak-power} reductions (kW) obtained under demand-charge tariffs, where the bill penalises coincident peak directly. Our metric is \emph{cost} (SAR) under the Saudi tiered \emph{volumetric} tariff, where the bill depends on cumulative kWh rather than peak kW. A large peak-reduction percentage and a modest cost-reduction percentage can describe the same physical control behaviour under different tariff structures; the two numbers are not commensurable. Reporting cost under the tariff that Saudi residents actually pay is a deliberate choice for external validity, and it necessarily yields smaller percentages than peak-based metrics.

\textbf{The baseline is already competent.} We benchmark against a hysteresis thermostat that performs sensible local on/off control, not a naive fixed schedule. The stronger the baseline, the smaller the headroom any coordinator can capture; a modest improvement over a competent baseline can represent equal or better absolute control than a large improvement over a weak one. We regard benchmarking against a realistic baseline as a matter of methodological honesty rather than a limitation to be minimised.

\textbf{The operating regime is deliberately adversarial.} We evaluate a strict comfort band ($23$--$25^\circ$C) in an extreme-heat climate, which is the realistic requirement for Saudi residential occupants and simultaneously the least favourable regime for load-shifting: the building operates near thermal saturation, leaving little feasible slack for the scheduler to exploit. Studies reporting larger savings frequently assume milder climates, wider comfort tolerances, or more thermally flexible buildings. Our smaller number is, in part, the price of realism---and quantifying that price is itself informative.

\textbf{The result is fully reproducible.} The complete simulation and training pipeline is released, so every reported number can be independently regenerated. We view reproducibility as a first-order quality signal that is frequently absent from higher-headline studies.

Taken together, these points support a specific and defensible claim: the primary contribution is not a record cost reduction, but a \emph{rigorous and honest characterisation} of what physics-informed coordination delivers for Saudi residential On/Off cooling---including a clear delineation of where it stops helping. The finding that strict-band cooling in extreme heat is near thermal saturation, so that the practical leverage lies in comfort flexibility and tariff structure rather than in coordination alone, is a genuine and, to our knowledge, previously unquantified result. The comfort-extension analysis, which shows that a $\pm1^\circ$C band widening roughly doubles to triples the achievable saving, provides an actionable design insight for policy and building operation in the Gulf climate.

We are explicit that this framing does not claim superiority in raw savings; where a study genuinely achieves larger cost reductions under a comparable tariff, baseline, and climate, that result stands. Our aim is to establish an honest and reproducible reference point for this specific and widely relevant setting.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations affect the scope and generalizability of these results. First, the first-order lumped thermal model with fixed parameters provides analytical tractability for the feasibility analysis but does not capture radiative heat transfer, furniture thermal mass, humidity effects, or occupancy-driven ventilation. Real buildings exhibit higher-order dynamics that would require system identification from measured data. Second, all results are simulation-based using a lumped-parameter RC thermal model driven by representative Jeddah diurnal profiles; no calibration against measured indoor temperatures or actual electricity bills has been performed, and the reported improvements should be interpreted as proof-of-concept results rather than deployment-ready predictions. Third, the four-month evaluation (January, April, July, October) captures the seasonal range but does not demonstrate month-to-month transitions; a continuous 12-month simulation would strengthen the robustness claim. Fourth, the top-$k$ projection is a heuristic that can produce transient comfort violations under the tight band in peak-heat conditions; formal constraint enforcement via constrained policy optimization~\cite{ref37} would provide stronger safety properties. Fifth, the extended comfort range [22--26]$^\circ$C requires occupant acceptance validation in Saudi households, which has not been conducted. Sixth, the framework is limited to On/Off units; inverter-driven variable-speed compressors would require continuous action spaces. Seventh, while the baselines include 8 methods, the framework is \textit{cost-effective} rather than provably \textit{cost-optimal}: no optimality certificate is established, and the MPC baselines use short horizons (1 and 3 steps). Comparison against a properly tuned MILP scheduler with a day-ahead horizon, longer-horizon MPC, and modern constrained RL approaches would be required to quantify the remaining optimality gap; this is identified as priority future work. Finally, coupling coefficients $K_{ij}$ were estimated rather than measured in situ.

\textbf{Idealizations as conservative assumptions.} It is worth noting the \emph{direction} in which the model's idealizations bias the reported savings. The omitted factors---occupancy heat gains, furniture and multi-surface thermal mass, humidity (latent load), and solar distribution---all tend to \emph{increase} the true cooling demand relative to the present model. A higher cooling demand pushes a building further toward thermal saturation under the strict band (where we already report the smallest savings) and, more importantly, further into the second tariff tier, where tariff-aware coordination has the most leverage to reduce cost. The idealized model is therefore, if anything, \emph{conservative} with respect to the tariff-crossing mechanism that drives the larger savings at building scale and under comfort extension: a fuller, higher-fidelity model incorporating these loads would plausibly show the coordination value to be at least as large as reported here, not smaller. We present this as a directional argument rather than a quantitative claim; verifying it with a detailed building-physics model is future work.

%%=========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%=========================================================================

This paper presented PI-PPO, a physics-informed deep reinforcement learning framework for cost-effective coordination of On/Off split AC units (18{,}500~BTU, 1.8~kW, EER~10.25) in Saudi residential buildings under the kingdom's two-tier tariff. Three features distinguish PI-PPO from prior work. First, it learns a \textit{stationary} policy that operates over an infinite time horizon, eliminating the per-step optimization of MPC and the reactivity of classical lazy scheduling. Second, the physics-informed reward improves comfort stability relative to reward-only baselines, though under a strict band in extreme heat some residual violations remain (mean magnitude on the order of 0.1--0.2$^\circ$C), as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:comfort}; this residual is an honest limitation rather than a claim of perfect comfort, in all tested scenarios---a property absent from standard DRL methods. Third, the model incorporates inter-zone thermal coupling, enabling the agent to exploit buffering through shared walls.

Evaluated across four representative months with Jeddah weather data, PI-PPO reduced July electricity cost by approximately 4\% for a 5-zone villa under the strict [23--25$^\circ$C] band, and by only about 1\% for a 20-zone building whose July demand approaches thermal saturation. Savings were consistently larger in the cooler and shoulder months (up to roughly 9\% in January) than in peak July---a clear seasonal pattern indicating that coordination delivers most where cooling demand is substantial but non-saturating, and least at peak demand when the building runs near its cooling capacity. This suggests the approach would be more effective in cooler Saudi locations, a hypothesis that follows from the observed seasonal trend and could be tested directly in future work. Under a strict band in extreme heat, coincident peak demand is largely unchanged because the comfort constraint forces compressors on; the savings therefore stem from energy and tariff effects rather than peak reduction. Extending the comfort band by $\pm$1$^\circ$C---justified by ASHRAE adaptive comfort standards and supported by the feasibility analysis, which shows that a wider band lengthens each zone's feasible OFF interval and thereby increases the scheduler's freedom to stagger compressor operation---yielded approximately 13.4\% savings for the villa and approximately 7.8\% for the building in July. The waterfall decomposition showed scheduling coordination (20.9~pp), PPO learning (4.1~pp), tariff awareness (4.0~pp), physics reward (2.0~pp), coupling (2.0~pp), and comfort extension (14.0~pp). The framework scaled to 100~zones with inference below 0.04~s.

Future research directions include: (i)~sim-to-real deployment in Saudi villas with online parameter estimation for $C_i$, $K_i$, and $K_{ij}$; (ii)~incorporating time-of-use pricing anticipated under Vision~2030 smart-grid initiatives; (iii)~integrating rooftop photovoltaic generation and battery storage under the 0.07~SAR/kWh feed-in tariff~\cite{ref16}; (iv)~occupant field studies validating acceptance of the 22--26$^\circ$C extended range in Saudi households; (v)~extension to inverter-driven variable-speed units requiring continuous action spaces; and (vi)~replacing the soft physics penalty with constrained policy optimization (CPO~\cite{ref37}) to provide formal comfort safety certificates.

\section{Detailed Training and Evaluation Procedure}
\label{sec:appendix}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\small
\SetAlgoLined
\caption{PI-PPO Training and Evaluation (Detailed)}
\label{alg:detailed}
\KwIn{Environment $\mathcal{E}$, surrogate $f_\phi$ (inputs incl.\ $\{K_{ij}\}$), weights $\omega_0\!\ldots\omega_5$, bounds $[l,h]$}
\KwOut{Trained policy $\pi_\theta^*$}
Initialize policy $\pi_\theta$, value function $V_\psi$\;
Pre-train surrogate $f_\phi$ on 50{,}000 Latin hypercube samples\;
\For{episode $= 1, \ldots, N_{\text{ep}}$}{
  Reset $\mathcal{E}$; $E_{\text{cum}} \leftarrow 0$; $(k, \{Q_i\}) \leftarrow f_\phi(T_a, \{C_i, K_i, Q_{\text{int},i}, l_i, h_i\}, \{K_{ij}\})$\;
  \For{$t = 0, \ldots, T_{\max}$}{
    Observe $\mathbf{s}_t = [\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}_{t-1}, T_a, E_{\text{cum}}, k, \Delta\mathbf{x}, t_{\text{hour}}]$\;
    $\mathbf{m}_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_t)$; project to $\|\mathbf{m}_t\|_1 \leq k$ via top-$k$\;
    Execute $\mathbf{m}_t$ in $\mathcal{E}$; observe $\mathbf{x}_{t+1}$\;
    $E_{\text{cum}} \mathrel{+}= 1.8 \cdot \|\mathbf{m}_t\|_1 \cdot \Delta T$\;
    Compute $r_t$ via Equation~\eqref{eq:reward}\;
    Store $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{m}_t, r_t, \mathbf{s}_{t+1})$\;
    \If{$|T_a^{\text{new}} - T_a| > 2^\circ$C}{$(k, \{Q_i\}) \leftarrow f_\phi(T_a^{\text{new}}, \{C_i, K_i, Q_{\text{int},i}, l_i, h_i\}, \{K_{ij}\})$}
  }
  Compute advantages $\hat{A}_t$ via GAE($\lambda$)~\cite{ref38}\;
  \For{epoch $= 1, \ldots, 10$}{
    $L(\theta) \leftarrow \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t[\min(\rho_t \hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(\rho_t, 1\!-\!\epsilon, 1\!+\!\epsilon)\hat{A}_t)]$\;
    Update $\theta, \psi$ via gradient ascent on $L(\theta) - c_v L_V + c_e H(\pi_\theta)$\;
  }
}
Evaluate all 3 seed policies; report mean and standard deviation\;
\textbf{Note:} the training loop above is run \emph{independently for each month} $m$, producing $\pi_{\theta,m}^*$\;
\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{--- Evaluation Phase ---}\;
\For{month $m \in$ \{Jan, Apr, Jul, Oct\}}{
  Load month-matched policy $\pi_{\theta,m}^*$; reset $\mathcal{E}$ with month $m$ weather\;
  $E_{\text{cum}} \leftarrow 0$; $P_{\text{peak}} \leftarrow 0$; $v_{\max} \leftarrow 0$\;
  \For{day $= 1, \ldots, 30$}{
    \For{$t = 0, \ldots, 95$}{
      $\mathbf{m}_t \leftarrow \arg\max \pi_\theta^*(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_t)$; project to $\|\mathbf{m}_t\|_1 \leq k$\;
      Execute in RC simulation; $P_t \leftarrow 1.8 \cdot \|\mathbf{m}_t\|_1$\;
      $E_{\text{cum}} \mathrel{+}= P_t \cdot 0.25$; $P_{\text{peak}} \leftarrow \max(P_{\text{peak}}, P_t)$\;
      $v_{\max} \leftarrow \max(v_{\max},\; \max_i \text{viol}_i)$\;
    }
  }
  $\text{Bill} \leftarrow 0.18 \cdot \min(E_{\text{cum}}, 6000) + 0.30 \cdot \max(0, E_{\text{cum}} - 6000)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}
